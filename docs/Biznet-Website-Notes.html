Where we stand

Most visitors decide in about ten seconds.

In that time they are trying to answer one question: can these people help me make a better decision about my project? If the answer isn't obvious, they leave.

A development consultancy doesn't sell square metres or a product you can click and buy. It sells judgement — the confidence that someone experienced will tell you whether a scheme stacks up before you commit capital, and then keep it on track once you do. That's a harder thing to prove on a webpage, which is exactly why the website has to work harder.

The competitors worth watching have all figured this out. They don't open with a list of services. They open with the decision they help you make, show the path they take you down, and then back it up with real work. Everything on this page is aimed at getting Biznet to the same standard, without claiming anything the firm doesn't genuinely do.

Sell the confidence first. The capability is what proves it — not the other way round.

What a better site needs

The seven things the site has to get right.

None of these is complicated. Together they turn a brochure into something that actually moves a serious client toward a conversation. For each one, here's the piece to build.

01

A hero that leads with the outcome

The first screen shouldn't say "welcome" or list what the firm does. It should say what Biznet gives a client, in plain language, with one clear next step.

Build it like this: a single line such as "Development clarity, from land to landmark", one supporting sentence, one button ("Let's talk"), and the three core disciplines named underneath — Project Development · Project Management · Feasibility. Nothing else competing for attention.
02

A plain account of how you work

Clients want to know the method before they trust the result. A short, four-step path does more than a paragraph of promises.

Step 01EvaluateObjectives, constraints and the real potential of the project.
Step 02ValidateHighest and best use, and whether the numbers actually work.
Step 03DirectProject definition, planning and coordination.
Step 04ControlMonitoring, reporting and oversight through delivery.
03

Service cards that lead somewhere

Each core service needs its own card on the homepage and its own detail page behind it. The card gives the one-line reason it exists; the page gives the scope, the outputs and the way in.

Build it like this: number the services and give each a short, confident description and an "Explore →" link. For example — Project Development: shaping the opportunity and the right direction before execution begins. Project Management: owner-side control across execution. Feasibility: financial and market clarity, highest and best use, modelling and return. Brand & positioning sits alongside as the fourth.
04

Routes by who the visitor is

An investor, a developer and a landowner are each weighing a different risk. The site should let each one find themselves quickly rather than reading everything.

Build it like this: a short "who we work with" band — Investors · Developers · Landowners · Private Clients — each leading to content framed around that reader's decisions. Same firm, four doors in.
05

Projects shown as proof, not photos

Imagery alone proves nothing. The work needs a consistent format so a reader can see exactly what Biznet did and for whom.

Build it like this: group projects by type — Investment & Development · Commercial · Private Villas & Buildings · Residential — and give every case the same spine: sector, location, Biznet's role, the stage, and the result. Only ever show permission-cleared work, and never describe someone else's project as your own.
06

Insights that show expertise

A small, genuine library of articles is the cheapest way to prove the firm knows its market — and the one that keeps earning attention long after it's published.

Build it like this: a News & Insights section with clear categories (News · Press Release · Article) and a date on everything. Write about feasibility, development planning and the UAE market — the questions clients actually ask — and pair each piece with a route to the relevant service. A short FAQ on the homepage does the same job for the quick questions.
07

An enquiry that qualifies, gently

The point of the site is a good first conversation. The contact step should be short, but it should learn one useful thing about who's asking.

Build it like this: open with "What best describes you?" — Developer · Landowner · Investor · New business — then just name, email and phone, and room to describe the project. That one question tells the team how to prepare before they ever pick up the phone.
How to lay it out

A simple structure that holds all of it.

Keep the main navigation short and let everything hang off it cleanly. Seven items is plenty.

HomeProposition and proof
AboutApproach, team, evidence
ServicesThe core disciplines
ProjectsCase work by type
SectorsExperience by asset
InsightsArticles and views
ContactThe enquiry route
Project DevelopmentDirection and project definition before execution begins.
Project ManagementOwner-side programme, cost, reporting and coordination.
Feasibility & ViabilityHighest and best use, market fit, modelling and return.

Brand & positioning and company formation sit as supporting services, not headline navigation. Add standalone audience or sector pages only where there's real, differentiated content to fill them.

The homepage, top to bottom

Purpose first, proof second, the ask last.

A reader should be able to scroll once and come away knowing what Biznet decides for them, how it works, that it has done it before, and how to start.

  1. Hero — the outcome, the three disciplines, one call to action.
  2. Who we are — a short, honest paragraph on what the firm actually does.
  3. How we work — the four-step path: evaluate, validate, direct, control.
  4. What we do — the service cards, each leading to its detail page.
  5. Projects — selected work by type, with the role made clear.
  6. Who we work with — the four client routes.
  7. Insights — three or four recent pieces, dated and categorised.
  8. Let's talk — the enquiry form that opens with "what best describes you?".
How to get there

Three stages, roughly a quarter of work.

No need to build everything at once. Get the structure and the core pages right first, then add the proof, then tune it.

STAGE 1 · WEEKS 1–4

Set the structure

Lock the three core services, write the hero and the detail pages, agree which projects can be shown and what each claim is based on.

STAGE 2 · WEEKS 5–8

Add the proof

Publish the case studies and leadership profiles, switch on the client routes, write the first three articles, and refine the enquiry form.

STAGE 3 · WEEKS 9–12

Tune and measure

Check mobile and load speed, sort the technical SEO, then watch how people actually use it and adjust the copy and forms.

How we'll know it's working

Four things worth counting.

Visitor numbers on their own tell you very little. These four say whether the site is doing its real job.

Qualified enquiriesMessages that name a service, a stage and a genuine project — not just traffic.
Service engagementHow far people read the service pages and whether they click through.
Search visibilityNon-branded searches that find Biznet, especially around feasibility and development.
Form completionHow many people who start the enquiry actually finish it.
The one thing to remember

Clarity of evidence beats volume of claims.

Biznet doesn't need the longest service menu or the biggest project list. It needs a site that states clearly what it helps clients decide, shows the method, and proves a few things properly. Do that, and the first conversation gets a lot easier to earn.
